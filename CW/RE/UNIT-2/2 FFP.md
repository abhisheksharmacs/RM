# Research Misconduct: Fabrication, Falsification, and Plagiarism (FFP)

## Definition of Research Misconduct

**Research misconduct** refers to unethical practices that violate the principles of honesty, integrity, and transparency in research. The three most serious forms of research misconduct are collectively known as  **FFP** :

1. **Fabrication** – Making up data or results.
2. **Falsification** – Manipulating research materials, equipment, processes, or data.
3. **Plagiarism** – Using another person's ideas, words, or work without proper acknowledgment.

Research misconduct damages the credibility of science, misleads other researchers, wastes resources, and can harm society.

---

# 1. Fabrication

## Definition

**Fabrication** is the act of  **inventing or creating data, observations, results, or experiments that never actually occurred** , and presenting them as genuine research findings.

### Simple Meaning

When a researcher writes fake data instead of collecting real data, it is called  **fabrication** .

### Scenario

A researcher is conducting a survey on the impact of online learning on students.

* Required sample size: 500 students
* Actual responses received: 200 students

Instead of collecting the remaining 300 responses, the researcher creates imaginary responses and enters them into the dataset.

The final report claims:

> "Data were collected from 500 students."

However, 300 responses never existed.

This is **fabrication** because the researcher manufactured data.

### Another Example

A scientist claims:

> "I conducted 50 laboratory experiments."

In reality, only 20 experiments were performed.

The remaining 30 results were completely invented.

This is fabrication.

### Why is it Wrong?

* Produces false scientific knowledge.
* Misleads future researchers.
* Can lead to incorrect decisions and policies.
* Destroys trust in research.

---

# 2. Falsification

## Definition

**Falsification** is the act of **manipulating, altering, omitting, or modifying research data, equipment, procedures, or results so that the findings appear different from the truth.**

### Simple Meaning

Changing real data to make results look better is called  **falsification** .

### Scenario

A researcher is testing a new medicine.

Actual results:

| Patient | Improvement (%) |
| ------- | --------------- |
| P1      | 10              |
| P2      | 12              |
| P3      | 8               |
| P4      | 11              |
| P5      | 9               |

The medicine does not appear very effective.

To make the medicine seem successful, the researcher changes the data:

| Patient | Reported Improvement (%) |
| ------- | ------------------------ |
| P1      | 40                       |
| P2      | 45                       |
| P3      | 38                       |
| P4      | 42                       |
| P5      | 41                       |

The researcher altered genuine data.

This is  **falsification** .

### Another Example

A researcher obtains 100 experimental observations.

Ten observations do not support the desired hypothesis.

The researcher secretly removes those ten observations and publishes only the favorable results.

This selective modification is falsification.

### Why is it Wrong?

* Distorts scientific truth.
* Leads to incorrect conclusions.
* Can result in harmful products or treatments.
* Undermines public confidence in research.

---

# 3. Plagiarism

## Definition

**Plagiarism** is the use of another person's words, ideas, data, images, tables, or intellectual work without proper acknowledgment or citation, while presenting it as one's own work.**

### Simple Meaning

Copying someone else's work and claiming it as your own is called  **plagiarism** .

### Scenario

A student finds the following paragraph in a journal article:

> "Artificial Intelligence has transformed healthcare by enabling early disease diagnosis and predictive analytics."

The student copies the exact sentence into a research paper without quotation marks or citation.

Readers believe the student wrote it.

This is  **plagiarism** .

### Another Example

A researcher copies:

* Literature review
* Figures
* Tables
* Research methodology

from another paper without giving credit.

This is plagiarism.

### Why is it Wrong?

* Violates intellectual property rights.
* Steals credit from original authors.
* Damages academic reputation.
* Can result in paper rejection, degree cancellation, or legal consequences.

# Easy Way to Remember FFP

### F = Fabrication

**"Fake Data"**

Example: Creating survey responses that never existed.

### F = Falsification

**"Fixing Data"**

Example: Changing experimental results to support a hypothesis.

### P = Plagiarism

**"Passing Others' Work as Your Own"**

Example: Copying text, figures, or ideas without citation.

# Exam-Oriented Conclusion (5 Marks)

**Fabrication, Falsification, and Plagiarism (FFP) are the three major forms of research misconduct. Fabrication involves creating fake data, falsification involves manipulating existing data, and plagiarism involves using another person's work without proper acknowledgment. These unethical practices violate research integrity, reduce the credibility of scientific findings, and can lead to serious academic, professional, and legal consequences. Therefore, researchers must maintain honesty, transparency, and accountability throughout the research process.**

=========================================================


Yes. Although **Fabrication, Falsification, and Plagiarism (FFP)** are the three broad categories of research misconduct, each can be further divided into common subtypes.

# 1. Fabrication (Making Up Data)

### a) Complete Fabrication

The entire dataset, experiment, or survey is invented.

**Scenario:** A researcher claims to have surveyed 1,000 students but never conducted any survey.

### b) Partial Fabrication

Some data are real, while the remaining data are artificially created.

**Scenario:** Out of 200 responses, only 120 are real and 80 are invented.

### c) Fabricated Observations/Experiments

Experiments or observations that never occurred are reported.

**Scenario:** Reporting results from laboratory trials that were never performed.

---

# 2. Falsification (Manipulating Data)

### a) Data Manipulation

Changing numerical values to obtain desired results.

**Scenario:** Changing a patient's blood pressure reading from 140 to 120.

### b) Data Omission (Selective Reporting)

Removing unfavorable observations.

**Scenario:** Excluding test results that contradict the hypothesis.

### c) Image Manipulation

Altering photographs, X-rays, microscopy images, ECG plots, etc.

**Scenario:** Enhancing a microscopy image to make a treatment appear more effective.

### d) Methodological Falsification

Misrepresenting research procedures.

**Scenario:** Claiming random sampling was used when convenience sampling was actually used.

### e) Statistical Falsification

Using inappropriate statistical methods intentionally to obtain significant results.

**Scenario:** Repeatedly testing data until a desirable p-value is found and reporting only that result.

---

# 3. Plagiarism (Using Others' Work Without Credit)

### a) Direct (Verbatim) Plagiarism

Copying text word-for-word without citation.

**Scenario:** Copying an entire paragraph from a journal article.

### b) Mosaic (Patchwork) Plagiarism

Combining copied phrases from multiple sources while making minor changes.

**Scenario:** Taking sentences from several papers and stitching them together.

### c) Paraphrasing Plagiarism

Rewriting another author's ideas in different words without citation.

**Scenario:** Changing sentence structure but keeping the same idea and not citing the source.

### d) Idea Plagiarism

Using another person's original idea without acknowledgment.

**Scenario:** Presenting a colleague's research concept as your own.

### e) Data Plagiarism

Using someone else's dataset without permission or citation.

### f) Image/Figure Plagiarism

Using graphs, diagrams, photographs, or figures without credit.

### g) Code Plagiarism

Copying software code, algorithms, or scripts without acknowledgment.

### h) Self-Plagiarism

Reusing one's own previously published work without proper disclosure.

**Types of Self-Plagiarism:**

* Duplicate publication
* Redundant publication
* Text recycling
* Salami slicing (publishing one study as many small papers)

### Quick Memory Trick

* **Fabrication = Create** (data that never existed)
* **Falsification = Change** (data that existed)
* **Plagiarism = Copy** (someone else's work)

A common exam question is:

> **"Differentiate between Fabrication, Falsification, and Plagiarism with examples."**

A one-line answer:

* **Fabrication:** Creating fake data.
* **Falsification:** Altering real data.
* **Plagiarism:** Stealing others' intellectual work.
